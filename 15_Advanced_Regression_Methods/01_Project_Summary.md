# When OLS Fails: Advanced Regression for Non-Standard Outcomes

Ordinary least squares regression is the default statistical tool in most research workflows, but it breaks down on a surprisingly common class of outcomes: event counts, ranked survey responses, samples with a selection ceiling, and outcomes bunched at a floor. When OLS is applied to these data, it produces biased coefficient estimates — predictors look weaker than they are, some effects reverse sign, and predictions fall outside the range of possible values. This project works through five regression models that handle each of these cases, demonstrating on real data where OLS goes wrong and how each specialised model corrects it.

## What Was Found

**Poisson vs. negative binomial for count data.** Academic award counts (N = 200 students) were right-skewed with 60% zeros. Poisson regression identified math score as the strongest predictor — each additional point was associated with a 7.3% increase in expected awards (IRR = 1.073, p < .001). Academic programme students received nearly three times as many awards as General programme students at the same math score (IRR = 2.96). Model comparison showed that despite the count structure, Poisson fit better than negative binomial (AIC 373.50 vs 383.97) because overdispersion was only mild (dispersion ratio 1.082). This is a useful calibration point: negative binomial is not automatically superior to Poisson — the data decide.

**Ordinal logistic regression for ranked survey responses.** Across 5,381 World Values Survey respondents, country of residence was the dominant predictor of perceived government poverty effort. US respondents were 85.5% more likely than Australians to endorse higher-effort categories (OR = 1.855, p < .001), while Swedish respondents were 45% less likely (OR = 0.547, p < .001). Age was a consistent positive predictor across all countries (p < .001). Treating this three-category ordered response as a continuous 1–3 numeric score would have forced equal interval assumptions and returned a single slope across all thresholds — concealing the country-level pattern that the ordinal model reveals.

**Truncated regression for restricted samples.** The scores dataset (N = 178) showed no observations at or above 100, indicating right-truncation at the ceiling. When the bulk of the data sits well below the truncation point (mean = 74.2), OLS and truncated regression produce similar estimates — a useful finding in itself. Truncation bias is most consequential when the selection cutoff falls within the central mass of the distribution.

**Tobit regression for censored outcomes.** Marriage happiness rating was the strongest predictor of extramarital affairs in a synthetic illustrative dataset (N = 601; 56% zero reports). The Tobit model estimated the rating effect at β = −1.11, more than double the OLS estimate of β = −0.53. Moving from the unhappiest to the happiest marriage rating reduced predicted affairs by 4.45 units under Tobit versus only 2.14 under OLS. The OLS line was systematically flattened by the mass of zero reports being treated as genuine data rather than censored observations. The same attenuation pattern appeared for religiousness (Tobit β = −0.53 vs OLS β = −0.24).

![Tobit vs OLS predicted affairs by marriage rating — Tobit correctly recovers the steeper relationship](docs/figures/fig07-tobit-predicted.png)

The figure above shows the central finding from the Tobit analysis: the OLS line (grey dashed) is shallower and ends at a positive value even at the highest marriage happiness rating, while the Tobit line (purple solid) is steeper and crosses zero between ratings 2 and 3, correctly reflecting that satisfied couples have near-zero latent propensity. The 2× difference in slope magnitude between the two models demonstrates why applying OLS to censored outcomes systematically understates the strength of real relationships.

## Practical Value

The five models together form a decision checklist for regression model selection. The choice is determined by one question before any modelling begins: what does the distribution of the dependent variable actually look like? A right-skewed count with many zeros points to Poisson or negative binomial. An ordered categorical response points to ordinal logistic. An outcome that is missing above or below a threshold points to truncated regression. An outcome bunched at a boundary with real observations recorded there points to Tobit. Default OLS misspecifies each of these structures in predictable ways, and the cost is not just statistical precision — it is the practical difference between correctly sizing an intervention and underestimating it by a factor of two.
